The lean was never robust, and the noise was not why

Session 17. S15 ended with a real-time controller and one sentence it could not defend: under 1.1° of noise on the initial joint angles it fell in two or three runs of five, and the page attributed that to tracking a plan from a single certified pose. That is not what was happening. The failing runs topple backwards, in the feet-only approach, before the bracing arm is anywhere near the table — and the reason is that the maneuver cannot be launched from rest. Fixing the launch takes the survival rate from 38% to 92%, and from 25% to 75% under a disturbance model that matches how this robot is actually instantiated. Six other things that look like the fix are measured and are not.

2026-08-14 · lean_analysis/croco_{robust,grid,plan,run,replay}.py · run dir lean_analysis/runs/2026-08-14_session17/ · follows the OpenMP build and the cost-stack page · crocoddyl 3.2.1 (OpenMP build), Pinocchio 4.1.0, 12 threads, H = 35, one DDP iteration, 20 ms control period

What changed in the measurement, before any result is read. Two things, and both make the numbers harder than S15's.
  • The plant now enforces the torque clamp. The command the robot takes is ctrl = qdes + (τff + kdvdes)/kp, so the servo's own PD term is added downstream of the planner's feedforward and can push a joint past the limit the plan was solved against. Measured on the S15 replay: 1.111 × the clamp basis, on the bracing arm (wrist yaw, shoulder yaw, elbow), in 4 of 200 control periods, all in the first 160 ms while the arm is being flung out. Every run below sets the actuator force range to the clamp basis, so the limit is enforced by the actuator instead of reported afterwards. It costs nothing nominally.
  • The disturbance model includes the base. S15 perturbed 27 joint angles and left the floating base exactly where the plan put it. That is not how this robot arrives — it is lowered on a winch, so where it lands, which way it faces and how level it is all vary. Joint-only noise moves the feet relative to the floor (12–25 mm of foot-corner spread at 0.02 rad, i.e. a drop test); base noise moves the whole robot relative to the table, which is where the certified landing spots are. The winch1 profile is 20 mm of horizontal placement, 10 mm of height, 50 mrad of heading, 20 mrad of tilt and the same 0.02 rad of joint noise.

1 · It fails backwards, and before the brace

The first thing to establish is where in the maneuver the failure is, because every fix S15 would have reached for is a fix to the braced phase. It is not there. Over the seeds that fall, the bracing arm never reaches the table: the elbow contact force stays at zero for the whole run while the CoM walks off the back of the foot polygon — at 0.98 s on the seed traced below — and the robot sits down. The support polygon at the stand keyframe runs from x = 0.185 to x = 0.388 and the CoM starts at x = 0.227, so the maneuver begins 42 mm from its own rearward stability boundary, before any noise at all.

Two controls narrow it further. The failure is not myopia: at H = 100 the MPC re-solves the entire remaining maneuver every 20 ms and the same seed still falls. It is not solver budget: at 2 and at 5 DDP iterations it falls too, and worse, with the line search collapsed to α = 0.002 and no step accepted — the signature of a problem whose descent direction is being spent on something the model cannot express. It is not the controller either: the offline Riccati gains and the pure feedforward fall on the same seeds.

That last observation is the important one, and it has a clean explanation. To a DDP whose feet are bilateral 6-D welds, the robot cannot fall over. The feet may pull and their centre of pressure may leave the sole; a toppling state is not in the model's reachable set at all. When the plant does topple, the measured state is off the model's own constraint manifold, and what the MPC returns from there is not a recovery — it is the projection of a fall onto a robot that is bolted to the floor.

2 · Six ways to price the fall that do not work

If the model cannot see a topple, the objective has to. Two terms are supposed to be doing that job and the weight sweep says what each is worth. Every row is a re-solved plan and a fresh set of replays under the same verdict.

The CoM barrier is inert, provably. It is a quadratic barrier on the foot polygon shrunk by 30 mm, and the plan cost at w = 1e3 and at w = 1e6 is identical to fifteen significant figures — 25.933907507070657 — because the nominal trajectory never leaves the box, so the term is on its flat branch at every node and its weight multiplies zero. The perturbed runs that do leave the box are already falling by the time it speaks. Its weight was never a robustness knob and neither is the margin: 30 mm and 50 mm give the same survival.

The cones are load-bearing and cannot be cranked. They are the only term that says the feet are unilateral and that the CoP has to stay on the sole, so the obvious move is to raise their weight until the plan stops asking for wrenches MuJoCo will not supply. It goes the wrong way: at wcone = 1e2 the controller falls in every seed and at 1e3 it falls from the nominal state. The cone residual is a stiff, strongly nonlinear function of the state through the KKT solve, and weighting it heavily buys a worse-conditioned problem rather than a safer trajectory. Demanding CoP margin directly instead — shrinking the sole rectangle the cone is written against to 60% — helps a little on its own and is undone by combining it with a heavier cone. A minimum normal force of 20 N does nothing.

Only one cost-side change works: an always-on CoM term. w_com_track is a plain quadratic pull of the horizontal CoM toward the middle of the feet on the feet-only phases, so there is a gradient before the excursion rather than a penalty after it, and at 1e4 it takes the five-seed screen to 100%. Its derivative counterpart does not: penalising horizontal centroidal momentum, which is where a topple shows up first and which ought to be the better signal, takes survival down to 33%. And §4 shows the CoM term is fixing a symptom.

3 · The maneuver cannot be launched from rest

The clue is in a control experiment that was supposed to be trivial. Command the stand keyframe and hold it:

feedforward, joints commanded to the keyframewhat the plant does
τff = 0 sags 0.09 rad in 0.2 s, topples backwards by 1.2 s
integral action until the joint error vanishes joint error 0.005 rad and it topples anyway — the joints were never the problem
the plan's own u[0] holds, but 74 N·m away from static: it settles 0.59 rad off the pose
the static-equilibrium QP at the start pose holds it: pelvis 0.995 m, CoM inside the polygon, for 3 s, at 0.34 of the clamp basis

So the pose is holdable and the plan's opening control is not a hold. Against a static torque of 53 N·m in norm, u[0] sits 74 N·m away from it — knees at −67 N·m where +10 is wanted, torso at −31 where 0 is wanted. The maneuver opens with a lurch, and whether it survives that lurch is decided by wherever the initial-state noise happened to put the robot. That is the two-in-five.

Two things about this are worth stating plainly. First, it explains why more optimisation made it worse and why a longer horizon did not help: neither changes the opening. Second, it explains the CoM term — pulling the CoM forward buys margin to absorb the lurch, which is why it works and why it is not the right fix.

The right fix is on both sides of the handover. n_hold gives the OCP the first 50 nodes (1 s) of the approach to sit still at x0, so the departure from rest is planned rather than assumed; it takes the opening mismatch from 74 N·m to 29 N·m and costs nothing in reach error (1.79 mm against 1.86 mm) or solve time. settle holds the start pose under the static-QP feedforward for 25 control periods before the schedule advances — which is what a stand controller is doing anyway while the winch lets go.

Neither half is sufficient. The settle alone, handed the original plan, is worse than no settle at all (it delivers the robot to the keyframe in equilibrium, which is precisely the state the violent opening is worst from). The planned departure alone gets 62%. Together they get 92% and 75%, and adding the CoM tracking term to them changes nothing — 92% and 75% again, which is the honest way to say that both were fixing the same failure and the launch is the better explanation of it.

A real defect found on the way, which is not the cause. The shipped stand keyframe does not seat the feet: each sole is rolled 6.4° (9 mm of corner-height spread across an 80 mm sole), so the robot stands on its two inner foot edges with the outer edges 4.4 mm in the air. seat_stance levels them with a five-variable Gauss-Newton on base height and the ankle roll/pitch pairs. It was the leading hypothesis for the backward topple and it is not the answer: the static hold from the seated pose topples identically. Worth fixing, worth not crediting.

4 · A diverse target set, and the stance that pays for it

Everything above is one reach target. The grid below is thirteen, spanning the axis this study has swept since S11 (x, straight ahead, out past where a brace stops helping) and the one it never has (y, across the full 595 mm width of the table, in both directions), plus two low targets 35 mm off the wood. Each is certified, planned and stressed at two stance offsets: the study's square-on dy = 0, and dy = −120 mm, the middle of the band croco_stance.py found useful in S14 and which no crocoddyl plan had ever used — croco_run read its start pose straight out of m.key_qpos and ignored the stance offset entirely, so the IK honoured it and the plan it fed did not. That is fixed.

5 · What this loop would actually need from a robot

The cone costs look like the obstacle to deployment, because their residual is a force and this robot has no force sensing worth the name. They are not: their wrench is what the contact forward dynamics' own KKT solve produces for (x, u), not a measurement — the MPC page now writes out all seventeen rows and where they come from. So the question is what is privileged here, and it is answerable one axis at a time: degrade one thing the controller is told, hold everything else, and score the same verdict.

Three of the four candidates are non-issues, and that is the useful half of the result. Joint sensing does not matter: 0.5 mrad of encoder noise with 50 mrad/s on the differentiated velocities — a fair model of what a 20 ms period actually gives you — costs nothing, 83% against 83%. Table pose does not matter up to 40 mm in either axis, which is well inside what any perception stack would deliver; the keep-out box and the landing spots are far more forgiving than they look. Touchdown timing is loose: the prescribed contact schedule can be advanced or retarded by ±0.2 s with little effect (only the earliest, −10 periods, drops to 50%), so a noisy skin used purely as a binary touchdown detector is more than accurate enough — which is the specific thing the experimental skins can do. And friction does not matter downwards: the plan assumes μ = 0.6 and survives μ = 0.3 unchanged.

Two things do. Friction matters upwards — at μ = 0.9 the controller falls in every seed, including from the nominal state, which is backwards from the intuition and is not yet understood. And the floating-base state estimate is the binding requirement of the whole loop:

Read as a specification, it is: base position to about 5 mm, base linear velocity to a few tens of mm/s, attitude to about 10 mrad, yaw to a few tens of mrad. Position is the tight one — 2 mm costs nothing but already moves the median reach error from 0.3 mm to 4.2 mm, 10 mm halves the survival, and 20 mm ends it. Attitude is looser than expected and degrades accuracy before it degrades survival: 10 mrad of roll/pitch keeps 83% and takes the reach error to 11.5 mm, and the split between roll/pitch and yaw matters less than the combined-estimator rows suggest. Angular rate at 10 mrad/s is free.

That is a good problem to have, and not the one this loop looked like it had. Nothing here needs a force estimate. What it needs is base position and velocity — and the feet are planted on the ground for the entire maneuver, which is exactly the regime in which leg odometry fused with an IMU is at its best. The requirement is demanding but it is the ordinary humanoid state-estimation problem, not a new sensor.

A correction, because it nearly became the headline. The first run of this table reported that 0.001° of attitude error toppled the robot, at identical reach error for every magnitude tried. A physical sensitivity has a slope; that one did not, which is what gave it away. The cause was pin.exp3(dr) * q.matrix() in the sensing model — numpy's * on two 3×3 arrays is element-wise, so it returned a non-orthonormal matrix that pin.Quaternion accepted without complaint, corrupting the attitude by an amount unrelated to dr. With @ the slope appears and is the table above. The disturbance profiles are unaffected — they build their rotation through MuJoCo's mju_mulQuat — so every other number on this page stands.

7 · The horizon, and the non-uniform version of it

The obvious remaining structural change is a fine head at the control period and a coarse tail at a multiple of it — a 1 s preview in 30 nodes instead of 50. It is not worth building here, and the reason is window management rather than models. crocoddyl offers exactly one cheap sliding operation, circularAppend, measured at 4.4 µs; it rotates the whole model list, which is only correct when every node has the same duration. A mixed window has to be re-pointed with updateModel, measured at 263 µs per node because it re-creates that node's data (createData alone is 98 µs). Re-pointing a fifteen-node coarse tail every control period is 3.9 ms against a ~10 ms step: the bookkeeping costs more than the fifteen nodes it removes.

What is cheap is the same idea on a uniform grid. Integrate the online problem at n times the control period and the window advances one coarse node every n periods instead of one fine node every period — rotation stays valid, the controller still re-solves from the measured state at 50 Hz, and the preview is n times longer for the same node count.

Two things come out of it, and neither is the one the idea was proposed for.

At equal preview the coarse grid is cheaper and does not work. 0.70 s of preview costs 11.1 ms at 35 nodes of 20 ms and 6.1 ms at 18 nodes of 40 ms — nearly half — and survival goes from 83% to zero, from the nominal state included. At 1.00 s it is 15.2 ms against 7.9 ms and 83% against 17%. So the saving is real and it is not a saving: what the controller needs is nodes, and below about 35 of them it does not matter how far ahead they see.

The loss is discretisation, not preview. The cleanest comparison in the table is 35 nodes against 35 nodes: at 20 ms each that is 0.70 s of preview and 83%, at 40 ms each it is 1.40 s of preview — twice as far ahead, same node count, same solver work — and survival drops to 67%. A 40 ms explicit Euler step through contact-constrained dynamics is simply a worse model of this plant, and more preview does not pay for it.

There is one cell where it earns its place. At 50 nodes the coarse grid gives 2.0 s of preview for 11.4 ms against 15.2 ms for 1.0 s at the fine grid, with survival equal at 83%. So if a future maneuver needs to see further than a second — a release-and-return phase, say — decimation buys that for 25% of the step rather than 100%. As a way to make the current controller cheaper it is a dead end, which is the honest answer to the question that started it.

9 · What is left

  • Yaw. If the split in §5 puts the failure in yaw rather than roll/pitch, this loop needs a heading source it does not have, and that is the first thing to fix — before any further tuning.
  • μ upwards. Higher friction than planned kills the controller and lower friction does not. That is backwards from the intuition and it is unexplained.
  • The 25% that still fall under the winch. They fall the same way — backwards, in the approach — so there is more of §3 left in them. The candidate is that the settle ends at a pose the plan's first node does not describe, and the plan should be re-solved from the settled state rather than assuming the keyframe.
  • Not the Delassus, still, and now not the horizon either.
