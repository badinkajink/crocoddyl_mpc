Why the closed loop fails

Session 14, a companion to the S13 page. That page ran a 7 × 4 grid of contact modes against reach targets, found that the plan holds the brace geometry to under a millimetre in every cell while the contact fails to establish in most of them, and concluded that the gap was contact establishment on curved geometry. That conclusion was built on three numbers — did it fall, how far did the hand miss, how much force went through the planned brace — and all three turn out to hide more than they show. Recomputing every cell from the trajectories that were saved gives a different and much less flattering account. In 11 of 17 cells the largest force on the table does not come from the brace at all; it comes from the torso, or from the reaching arm, or from a gripper nobody planned to put down. In 11 of 14 certified poses the brace sits 0–2 mm in from the lateral edge of a slab 595 mm wide — hooked over the rail, not resting on the wood — and the certification stage is what put it there. Three of the five topples happen before the contacts switch on, so they are failures of the approach and not of the brace. And the palm brace, which had the best contact duty in the grid, is made against 11 mm of collision proxy that the CAD does not support. Fixing the proxies against the CAD produces the study's first genuinely correct braced lean — one cell, at a target legs-only cannot reach, with 92 N through the planned site, 78% duty and the load 92 mm inboard — and collapses most of the rest.

This page revises the S13 grid, it does not replace the S13 maneuver. The headline result of §1–§9 there — a single target, single mode, stand → lean & reach → return that survives MuJoCo under MPC — still stands and still reproduces. What is revised is §11–§12 of that page, the claim about how far it generalises and the reason it does not, and the collision model everything on both pages was measured against. Everything below is simulation and nothing here has been near hardware.

1 · The grid, scored on what the plant did

The S13 sweep reported a cell as fell / reach_mm / brace_N. Each of those is doing less work than it looks like:

The numberWhat it actually measuresWhat it hides
mpc_brace_N The sum of normal forces on contacts whose body is the site's body. Everything else touching the table. A cell holding itself up on its chest reports 0 N of brace, which is indistinguishable from a cell hanging in free space — and the two do not have the same fix.
worst_penetration min(contact.dist) over table contacts. For the MESH links — which is exactly what the elbow and forearm braces are — MuJoCo's convex narrowphase does not return a usable depth. Against an exact vertex-vs-box SDF it under-reports by an order of magnitude, and at an inflated margin it returns numbers like −304 mm for an arm resting on the surface.
fell Pelvis below 0.55 m at any point. When. Three of the five S13 topples are already down at node 90; the contacts do not switch on until node 120. Those are failures of the approach — of getting from a stand into the pre-brace pose on the legs alone — and no amount of contact modelling touches them.
reach_mm at one instant Hand-to-target error at the end. Whether the plant arrived and let go, never arrived, or was thrown across the room. Values of 2.7–3.3 m appear in this grid.

croco_why.py recomputes each cell from the two trajectories that were saved — the OCP's own xs and the replay's qpos trace — and measures, per node, the exact signed distance from each brace surface to the table box, every table contact with its force and its position on the slab, and the reach error for both the plan and the plant. Nothing is modelled; it is all measurement of trajectories that already exist.

The pattern in that figure is the whole page in one line: every cell that reaches accurately is at x ≤ 1.05, which is exactly the range where legs-only also reaches. At x = 1.15 and 1.25 — the targets that actually need a brace — the best result is 49 mm short and the rest are 120, 186, 256 mm short or on the floor. The brace closes the loop only where it is not needed.

4 · The stall, and the topple that happens first

The far targets never converge. x = 1.25 gets to within 66 mm at node 170 and then backs away to 186 mm, and it does that while its plan sits at 17 mm. That shape is not tracking error in the usual sense — it is the controller declining to commit the CoM to a lean it has no support for, which is the correct thing for it to do and the reason the maneuver is circular: the plant will not lean without the brace, and the brace does not arrive until it leans.

Three shapes, three stories. 1.05 elbow is flat on zero: the brace is there, on the top face, and stays there. 1.05 palm sits 3–6 mm clear of the slab for the whole phase and still carries 7–8 N — because what is touching is the gripper box hanging over the lateral edge, catching the rail on a face rather than resting on the surface, and the geom carrying that load is *_gripper_collision, the box §5 shows is 12.5 mm bigger than the hardware. 1.15 elbow+palm climbs away from the table for the whole phase and ends 70 mm clear while the plan holds it at contact — the arm is being lifted off, because the chest has taken the load and the arm no longer has anywhere to be.

And the failure that is not a brace failure at all: in 1.250 forearm+palm the pelvis is at 0.375 m by node 90 and 0.16 m by node 110. The contacts switch on at 120. The robot never gets to the brace.

5 · The collision proxies are not the hardware

Every contact in this study is between the tabletop and a collision proxy — a capsule or a box standing in for a link whose real shape MuJoCo would rather not collide against. Each was added for a reason and each was verified in one direction. The brace loads them in another.

That is the same order as the entire inboard margin of most of the certified poses, and it is why the palm modes had the best contact duty in the grid.

The gripper has no collision mesh at all, and that is worth saying out loud. The MJCF's h12_mount mesh is only the 63 × 63 × 25 mm H12 adapter — the gripper body and fingers have no mesh in this model. The boxes are not a convex simplification of geometry that is present; they are the only geometry there is, hand-transcribed from CL_Assets' part STLs. brace_surfaces.py re-derives them from those STLs and prints the primitives the CAD supports, so the transcription is checked rather than trusted.

7 · The one cell that is a brace

Applying all three tests at once — did it reach, did the planned brace carry the load, was the load on the slab rather than the rail — exactly one of the 22 CAD-faithful cells passes.

What the good cell is doing that the others are not. Its brace surface is the raw upper-arm mesh hull, so there is no proxy to be wrong about. In the plant the load actually lands on the forearm link, 83 mm inboard, at 89 N — the "elbow" mode is in practice an elbow-and-forearm contact, which is the S11 observation that the elbow site sits ~47 mm from where the arm really touches, showing up again in closed loop. And it is one of only two certified poses in the entire grid whose brace was on the slab to begin with.

8 · The stance was never a variable

The rail contact raises an obvious question the study has never asked: the robot's stance is pinned at y = 0 in every run on both pages. The reach target is at y = −0.235, 60 mm from the far rail, so reaching it twists the torso and swings the bracing arm out to the near one. Is the rail brace kinematically forced, or is it an artifact of where we chose to stand? contact_select.STANCE_DY translates the whole robot, feet included, before the IK runs, so the question is one sweep:

9 · What this says about the pipeline

StageWhat it is blind toWhat would fix it
Certification (contact_select.solve_ik + the static QP) The table's edges. Non-penetration is written against the box, so a rail contact and a slab contact are the same constraint; nothing asks a brace to be inboard, and nothing notices when q* already has the torso on the wood. A support-region term on the brace sites — the same LP the CoM region already uses, applied to the contacts instead of the CoM — plus an explicit inboard margin in the admissibility test. Cheap, and it invalidates 11 of 14 currently-certified poses, which is the point.
Certification, again Its own tolerances. IK_MARGIN = 25 mm inflates every collision margin so the IK can see contacts early (necessary), and PLACE_TOL = 2 mm then accepts a 2 mm air gap as "placed". S13 fixed the 25 mm version of this; the 2 mm version is still there. Score placement at zero margin, as the press step already does, and require penetration rather than proximity.
The OCP Everything that is not a prescribed contact. The approach phase has to reach the pre-brace pose on its legs with a CoM barrier and no support — which is where 3 of 5 pre-fix and 7 of 11 CAD-faithful topples happen — and the braced phase's contacts are bilateral, so the plan may lean on a contact that can pull. Contacts the plan can acquire rather than assume: a deliberate press phase, or complementarity. And a keep-out on the reaching arm, which in this grid is repeatedly the largest contributor of table force.
The plant model Nothing, now — but it was wrong by 11 mm in the direction the palm brace loads, 6 mm in the direction the forearm brace loads, and by 0 mm at the elbow, which is the only brace that worked. Done (§6). The general lesson is that a proxy is only verified in the direction it was checked, and a brace loads links in directions nobody was thinking about when the proxy was added.
The experiment design The stance. One value, never varied, and it is the one that puts the brace on the rail and costs 3× the joint effort. Add Δy to the grid. §8 says the payoff is large and the cost is a sweep.

None of this touches the S13 conclusion that closing the loop is what makes the maneuver survive, or the C++ keep-out, or the crocoddyl formulation. It is all upstream of them: the pipeline was producing poses that certify, plan and track beautifully, and that a real table would not support.
