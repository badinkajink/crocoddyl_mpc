Making the crocoddyl MPC real-time: the cost stack was the wrong axis

Session 15. The S13 controller re-solves a 50-node BoxFDDP problem every control step and takes 77.7 ms doing it on this machine, against a 20 ms control period — 13 Hz where the loop needs 50–100. S13 attacked it by making the box keep-out activation native, S14 by asking whether the Delassus matrix was the bottleneck (it is 2.3% of a node). Both were optimising the cost of individual operations. This page measures the axis neither of them looked at — the number of cost terms — and ends at 12.2 ms, p95 16.1 ms, 82 Hz, inside the control period.

crocoddyl 3.2.1, Pinocchio 4.1.0, conda-forge, one Intel(R) Core(TM) Ultra 7 265KF (20 threads), commit 121c97d2b. Node timings are best-of in microseconds; MPC step times are means over the 200 control steps of a full 4 s MuJoCo replay, because there the question is what a control period costs in practice. nv = 33, nu = 27, nx = 66. Everything below is regenerated from runs/2026-08-13_session15/ by lean_analysis/croco_speed_page.py.

The short version. A crocoddyl cost term costs 1.61 µs per node before it computes anything, because CostModelSum accumulates a private 6,960-double Lxx/Lxu/Luu block for every term whether or not that term is active. The S13 node carried 98 terms, 86 of them keep-out points that are inactive at almost every node — 184 µs, 57% of the node, to enforce a constraint that is satisfied. Fusing them into one term that skips its inactive points is the same optimisation problem (checked to 1e-12) and halves the step. With three further changes the controller runs at 82 Hz: 6.4× over the published S13 controller and 16.7× over S12. Two plausible optimisations were measured and rejected.

1. Where S13 and S14 left it

S14 profiled a braced node and found the contact dynamics were 26–29% of it, with the Delassus matrix — the object the matrix-free literature is about — at 2.3%. It attributed the other three-quarters to "the cost stack, overwhelmingly the 86 box keep-out residuals" and stopped, because the question it had set itself was about the Delassus.

That attribution is right, and it is right for a reason S14 did not state and would not have predicted from the arithmetic. The keep-out is a box signed-distance function on a point: a handful of comparisons, a norm, and a 3 × nv Jacobian. The state regulariser next to it in the same cost sum is a 66-row residual over the whole state. If cost terms cost what they compute, the keep-out points should be the cheap ones.

The first thing to establish, then, is what a cost term costs when it computes nothing.

2. What a cost term costs before it computes anything

CostModelSum::calcDiff loops the terms and, for each active one:

data->Lx  += w * d_i->Lx        (n x   = 66)
data->Lu  += w * d_i->Lu        (n u   = 27)
data->Lxx += w * d_i->Lxx       (66 x 66)
data->Lxu += w * d_i->Lxu       (66 x 27)
data->Luu += w * d_i->Luu       (27 x 27)

That is 6,960 doubles of dense read-multiply-accumulate per term per node, over a CostDataAbstract that every term owns privately — and it happens whether the term's residual is zero, whether its activation is on its flat branch, and whether it depends on u at all. It is a property of the container, not of the cost.

Measured directly: N identical copies of the same cost in one CostModelSum on the real state, timed against N, for two costs that share none of their arithmetic.

A 3-row box keep-out point costs 2.20 µs and a 1-row control residual — which computes essentially nothing; its Jacobian is the identity — costs 1.61 µs. The slopes differ by 0.6 µs. About 73% of what a keep-out point costs is the container, not the constraint.

And the memory half, which the timing table does not show. Every term's private Lxx/Lxu/Luu is 6,960 doubles, so the S13 node carries 268 MB of cost data over a 50-node horizon against 31 MB after. That is why a node measured in isolation and a node measured inside a sweep disagree: isolated and cache-warm the S13 braced node is 323 µs, but the same node inside problem.calcDiff over 50 of them is 550 µs. Allocating the datas for the 200-model problem takes 565 ms against 201 ms — the reason the MPC class was already careful never to rebuild its problem.

4. One cost term for the whole keep-out

croco_ext/keepout.cpp gains CostModelBoxKeepOut, a CostModelAbstract that owns all 86 points. It is written against the cost base rather than as a residual + activation pair deliberately: a residual is the right abstraction when crocoddyl's Gauss-Newton assembly should do the work, and here the entire objective is to not do that work. The term writes Lx and Lxx itself, so:

  • one accumulation into the shared Lxx instead of 86;
  • one CostDataAbstract instead of 86;
  • and each point whose activation is zero costs one SDF evaluation — about 20 flops — instead of a frame Jacobian and an nv × nv Gauss-Newton block. Over the planned trajectory at most 7 of the 86 points are ever active at once, and the whole set is inactive at the certified pose by construction (the thresholds are taken there).
The bug this formulation has and the per-point stack could not. crocoddyl's contact forward dynamics calls pinocchio::computeAllTerms, which does not update frame placements — ResidualModelFrameTranslation::calc updates the one frame it owns, so 86 separate residuals each refreshed their own. A single term looping over oMf reads whatever the last residual to touch it wrote. The symptom is not a crash: the active set comes back empty at every state and the keep-out silently stops constraining anything, while the solve gets faster and still converges. It was caught by the equivalence test below, which is the only reason it is a footnote and not the result.

croco_ext/test_keepout.py builds the real action model both ways — 86 CostModelResidual terms against the one fused term, same contacts, same actuation, same damping — and compares cost, Lx and Lxx at 40 states drawn from the planned trajectory, 39 of which have a non-empty active set. They agree to 2e-18, 1e-14 and 2e-12. This is the same objective, not a similar one.

The offline solve goes 31 s → 13.4 s → 6.6 s across the three implementations, and the plan is the same plan: the brace lands within 0.04 mm of the same spots, the hand within 0.04 mm of the same target, and the pose within 0.011 rad of the same q*. The fused run takes 47 DDP iterations where the other two take 40, because accumulating one weighted sum instead of 86 reassociates the arithmetic and puts the solver on a marginally different iterate sequence. What has to match is the answer, and it does.

The braced node goes from 323 µs to 131 µs, and in situ over a 50-node sweep from 550 to 161 µs per node — a bigger factor than the isolated measurement, because the working set collapsed too. The contact dynamics are now 60% of the node, which is where S14 said the floor was.

5. The last interpreted object in the loop

The plant's passive joint torques (MJCF damping and friction loss, without which the plan's torques are systematically low on every moving joint) were a Python subclass of ActuationModelAbstract. Its calcDiff allocated a fresh nv × nx zero matrix and a np.diag per node per sweep:

                              calc      calcDiff
python passive             2.00 µs      4.68 µs
c++ passive                0.24 µs      0.25 µs
crocoddyl ActuationModelFloatingBase (no passive terms)   0.16 µs

croco_ext/actuation.cpp transcribes it, keeping the constant structure (dtau_du, the torque transform) written once in createData instead of rebuilt per call. It is worth 2–3 ms of the step and it matters more than that suggests for two reasons: calc is on the line-search path, where FDDP rolls the whole horizon out once per trial step; and it removes the last interpreter object from the node, which is the precondition for ever using ShootingProblem.nthreads — inert in this conda-forge build, which is compiled without OpenMP, but a Python model in every node would make that knob a GIL contention problem rather than a speed-up. croco_ext/test_passive.py checks the two agree on tau, dtau_dx, dtau_du and commands to 7e-15, at velocities spanning the tanh knee.

6. What is left in a step

With the node at 131 µs, a warm-started 50-node step at one iteration divides as:

solver.calcDiff  (one calc + one calcDiff sweep)   7.7 ms
backwardPass     (Riccati + BoxQP, 50 nodes)       4.6 ms
one line-search rollout                            1.00 ms  x 4 trials (median)

FDDP's forward pass is a full nonlinear rollout per trial step and the median accepted step is 0.125–0.1875, i.e. about 4 rollouts thrown away per iteration. So the step is roughly 45% derivative sweep, 25% backward pass, 25% line search — and the second DDP iteration costs all of that again, which is what the iters knob buys and sells.

That is not the usual shape of an accuracy/speed trade and it is worth being careful about: §9 shows the survival difference is not resolvable, so the honest claim is that one iteration is twice as fast and not measurably worse, not that it is better.

And the cones are load-bearing. Dropping the five friction/wrench cone costs from the online problem takes the braced node from 131 to 96 µs, and lets enable_force go off with them — they are the only costs that read the contact-force derivatives — for 84 µs, a 36% cheaper node. The controller then falls in most cells of the grid. The cones are 27% of the node and they are the last thing to remove.

8. One optimisation that looked obvious and is not

The p95 step is consistently ~40% above the mean, and the reason is the line search: a rung of crocoddyl's alpha ladder is a full rollout, the ladder has ten rungs, and a step that walks to the bottom costs ten of them. Truncating the ladder bounds the rollouts per iteration, which is exactly the shape a real-time budget wants. It does not work:

It saves no time — a rejected ladder still evaluates every rung it has, so the mean is flat or worse — and it destabilises the controller: with the ladder capped, a step that would have been taken at a small alpha is not taken at all, the regularisation rises, and the next period starts from a worse candidate. At H = 35 it falls at every cap tried. The real fix for the p95 is a cheaper rollout, which is what §4 and §5 are.

9. The robustness is not a speed problem

All of the above is measured on the deterministic replay. Repeating the promising cells with 0.02 rad (1.1°) of Gaussian noise on the initial joint angles, five seeds each:

Hitersseedsfellreach err mm, survivors
50253 / 51.4, 2.4
50152 / 51.8, 2.5, 9.5
35253 / 53.6, 4.5
35153 / 53.7, 7.0

Every configuration falls in two or three of five, and the differences between them are not resolvable at this sample size. That is a real limitation of this controller and it is worth stating plainly rather than burying: the horizon and iteration knobs can be chosen on speed alone in this range because none of them is buying robustness. Whatever fixes the 1.1° fall rate is a different piece of work — the plan is tracked from a single certified pose with no re-planning of the contact schedule, and a perturbed initial state is outside what the offline solve considered.

Two cells are defensible endpoints. At H = 50 the controller runs at 55 Hz (18.2 ms, p95 25.5 ms) with a 1 s preview and the same behaviour as S13. At H = 35 it runs at 82 Hz (12.2 ms, p95 16.1 ms) with a 0.7 s preview, a 3.6 mm reach error, and — the number that decides whether this is a controller — a p95 inside the 20 ms control period, which no previous session's configuration achieved even on its mean.

The shorter horizon is not free, and the cost is not in the reach error. Worst table penetration goes from 2.6 mm at H = 50 to 9.8 mm at H = 35: with 0.7 s of preview the arm arrives at the slab with less warning and the keep-out has to work harder against momentum it can no longer see coming. 9.8 mm is inside the soft-contact penetration MuJoCo already allows at these forces and well short of anything structural, but it is the number to watch if the horizon goes shorter still, and it is why H = 50 at 55 Hz is the configuration to prefer unless the extra headroom is needed — it is already real-time on the mean, and its p95 misses the period by 5.5 ms.

11. What would come next, and what would not

  • Not the Delassus, still. It was 2.3% of the S13 node and the node has since lost 60% of its cost elsewhere; it is a larger fraction of a smaller number and the same conclusion.
  • Threading. ShootingProblem.nthreads exists and is inert: this conda-forge crocoddyl reports multithreading support is not enabled. A build with OpenMP would parallelise the calcDiff sweep and the rollouts across nodes, which is 70% of the step, on a 20-thread machine. §5 removed the blocker on the crocoddyl side.
  • A non-uniform horizon. The cheapest remaining structural change: a fine head at 20 ms and a coarse tail at twice that keeps a 1 s preview in ~30 nodes instead of 50. ShootingProblem.updateModel makes it implementable without rebuilding the problem, but the coarse tail's models cannot be rotated with circularAppend, so the window management is real work rather than a flag.
  • The contact-dynamics floor is now 60% of a node (79 µs) and is crocoddyl's own DifferentialActionModelContactFwdDynamics. Below it there is no more accounting to do; there is a different action model.
