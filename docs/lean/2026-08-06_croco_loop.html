The lean survives MuJoCo, and the loop is what does it

Session 13. The S12 page ended with a crocoddyl plan that solved and did not survive MuJoCo, and blamed the open loop. Half of that was right. This page fixes three modelling defects the S12 diagnosis had not found — the braced phase was leaning on thin air, the table was a half-space instead of a box, and the Pinocchio model was missing 10 N·m·s/rad of joint damping the MJCF has on every joint — and then runs the whole controller ladder instead of two rungs of it. The result is a MuJoCo-feasible stand → lean & reach → return. Under a receding-horizon crocoddyl MPC the hand lands 2.5 mm from the target with the brace carrying 93 N, the CoM never leaves the support polygon, and the robot stands back up. Open loop, on the same plan, it falls over — as does the robot doing nothing at all, in 1.3 s. It then asks how far that generalises, over a grid of seven contact modes × four reach targets, and the answer is narrower than the headline: bracing becomes necessary somewhere between x = 1.05 and 1.15 m, the plan holds the brace geometry to a millimetre in every cell — and the contact still fails to establish in most of them.

Read this first. This is a simulation result and nothing here has been near hardware. The headline maneuver in §1–§9 is a single target, contact mode and stance, and §11–§12 exist precisely to say how representative it is: across a 7 × 4 grid it is the best cell rather than a typical one. The MPC runs at 38 ms per step against a 20 ms control period, so "MPC works" here means "closing the loop is what makes the maneuver survive" and "this is a good offline trajectory generator", not "this ships".

1 · What S12 actually got wrong

S12 reported two failures — the plan drove the bracing gripper 68 mm through the tabletop, and no replay established brace contact — and attributed both to the missing tracking controller. Walking the plan node by node through MuJoCo's narrowphase (croco_diag.py, new) says otherwise. Three separate defects, none of which a controller can fix:

DefectEvidenceWhy it was invisible
The braced phase was leaning on thin air. Over all 41 braced nodes the elbow site sits +9.7 mm above its certified landing height and the forearm −14.6 mm below it — one link hovering, the other buried. The report measured distance to the certified spot (35 mm, in 3-D) and never resolved it into the one axis that decides whether a contact exists.
The table was a half-space, and could not be one. At the S12 certified pose the gripper's lowest collision corner (left_gripper_jaw_a) sits 7.4 mm below the tabletop plane. Any z ≥ ztable barrier tight enough to exclude the gripper forbids the pose the study certified. Identified in S12 §6.1 and recorded as future work rather than as the reason the penetration number could not improve. Its explanation does not reproduce: that corner is at x = 0.851 m, well inside the table's 0.50–1.68 m footprint, so it is not "hanging over the edge where there is no table" — it is 7.4 mm inside the slab. The certified pose rests the gripper on the table and always did.
A body that was in contact was not a contact. At q*, MuJoCo reports the bracing gripper on the table — three contacts, 0.9 to 3.7 mm deep — alongside the elbow and forearm links. The mode being planned was elbow+forearm. The mode was chosen to match the MJPC strategy for a like-for-like comparison. That made the comparison fair and the plan wrong: the gripper was unconstrained and went through the slab.

The repairs are, respectively: a z-weighted landing cost at 100× the old weight plus an explicit hold term through the braced phase (ContactModel3D with Baumgarte Kp = 0 constrains velocity, not position — once the phase starts the site keeps whatever height it arrived at); a box signed-distance keep-out built from table_top_collision itself, with each point's threshold taken at q* so the certified pose costs exactly zero; and planning the mode the pose actually uses, elbow+forearm+palm.

A fourth thing, found while fixing the third. Excluding contact bodies from the keep-out — "their contact model already places them on the table" — is wrong, because the contact only exists during the braced phase. With the gripper excluded on those grounds the approach drove it 44 mm through the slab in the first 0.1 s. Because every keep-out threshold is taken at q*, a contact body's own keep-out reads "do not go deeper than the certified pose does", which the braced phase satisfies by construction. Deepest robot–table penetration over the plan, both measured here: −38.7 mm (S12) → −13.1 mm (S13).
One S12 number does not reproduce. S12 reported the deepest penetration as −67.8 mm on the left_magpie_gripper; re-measuring the bit-identical S12 trajectory here gives −38.7 mm on left_wrist_roll_link, and it is the same under a 0 mm and a 25 mm collision margin. The likely cause is the MuJoCo version — S12 ran 3.10.0, this runs the 3.2.3 the whole HAMS stack pins to match the MJPC ABI, and the mesh collision routines changed in between. Every before/after number on this page is measured on this machine with the same binary on both sides, which is why the comparison survives the discrepancy.

2 · The number that reframes the whole S12 conclusion

Before asking whether a plan survives MuJoCo, it is worth asking what survives MuJoCo. Put the robot at the stand keyframe, command that pose, and let go:

The robot is an inverted pendulum and the actuators are joint-level position servos. No joint-space command holds it, because none of them can see the base. Every open-loop replay in S12 was racing a 2.7 second clock, and the 2 s plan that "stayed upright" in the torque mode stayed upright because it ran out of trajectory first. That is not a property of the plan.

It also sets the bar for everything below: a controller that keeps the robot up for 4 s has already done something that the plant does not do on its own, and any comparison that omits the do-nothing row is flattering somebody.

3 · The two models: what the MJCF has that a URDF cannot hold

crocoddyl plans on a Pinocchio model built from h1_2_magpie.urdf. Every other number this study has produced came out of a MuJoCo model built from Lean_H12_Magpie.xml. Those are two importers' readings of the same CL Assets source, and a plan computed on one and executed on the other is worth exactly as much as the agreement between them. So the bridge (croco_bridge.py) is a module with a parity test attached rather than index arithmetic inline in the planner, and this section is the list of everything it has to reconcile — because two of the entries were the difference between "the open loop cannot work" and a maneuver that survives.

3.2 · The difference that actually decided the result

S12's bridge reconciled mass — 0.78 kg of back_equipment and gripper the URDF had no link for. It did not reconcile anything the MJCF puts on a joint, and the reason is structural rather than an oversight: a URDF has nowhere to write these. They are simulator parameters, not kinematics, so the format that crocoddyl's model comes from cannot carry them and nothing warns you that they are missing.

MJCF joint propertyValue (all 27 joints)What it is worth at the plan's velocities Where it can live in Pinocchio
damping10 N·m·s/rad up to 26 N·m per joint at 2.6 rad/s — more than a wrist's entire clamp budget, 54% of an ankle's nowhere. model.damping exists and no forward-dynamics algorithm reads it — aba/rnea ignore it entirely.
armature0.1 kg·m² against arm-link inertias of order 0.01–0.05 kg·m²: not a rounding correction to the mass matrix model.armature, which crba adds to the diagonal. Set it and the mass matrices agree.
frictionloss0.2 N·m small, but systematic and on every moving joint nowhere, and it is a non-smooth constraint in MuJoCo besides

Armature therefore goes into the model. Damping and friction go into the actuation model instead — a Python ActuationModelAbstract whose calc is

τ = [ 06 ; u − b·vj − f·tanh(vj/ε) ] ,    ε = 0.05 rad/s

with the matching dτ/dx = −diag(b + f·sech²(v/ε)/ε) on the joint-velocity block. Two consequences worth stating plainly. First, u now means the torque the actuator has to produce, not the net joint torque, which is the quantity the torque limits are about and the quantity the replay feeds forward. Second, the tanh is a stand-in: MuJoCo's frictionloss is a hard constraint and a DDP needs a differentiable model, so ε = 0.05 buys Lipschitz continuity through zero at the price of being ~1% low above 0.15 rad/s. It is 0.2 N·m of modelling error; leaving it out instead was a systematically low torque on every moving joint, and systematic error is what a feedforward term cannot absorb.

The plan was being computed for a robot that does not exist — one whose joints spin freely — and the missing term is larger than most of the torques the plan is choosing between.

3.3 · What the bridge does not reconcile, on purpose

Three differences are left standing because they are the point of the experiment rather than bugs in it, and the whole replay pipeline exists to measure what they cost:

  • Contacts are rigid and prescribed in crocoddyl, soft and discovered in MuJoCo. crocoddyl's DifferentialActionModelContactFwdDynamics solves a KKT system with the contact as an equality; MuJoCo integrates a compliant contact with solref/solimp and finds its own contact set every step. Planned forces will not equal measured forces — §5 measures the gap rather than assuming it away.
  • The actuators are position servos in MuJoCo and torque sources in crocoddyl. Every rung of the ladder converts, exactly, via ctrl = qdes + (τff + kdvdes)/kp.
  • The table's legs are not modelled in the OCP at all — only the top box. Nothing in this maneuver goes near them, and croco_diag.py checks that rather than asserting it.

The KKT damping is not a nuisance parameter. Two 6-D feet plus three 3-D brace contacts is 21 constraint rows on 33 DOFs, and the contacts are redundant: many force splits between feet and brace produce the same motion, so the KKT system is singular and the force the cone costs differentiate is arbitrary. The cone costs, and what they read

These two rows carry more of the result than their weight of 1e1 suggests, and they are the only costs in the stack whose residual is a force — so it is worth being exact about what that force is and where it comes from. For a foot the residual is the 17 rows of the contact wrench cone evaluated on the 6-D wrench at the sole:

wrench cone, per foot

r=Awc,wc=(fx,fy,fz,τx,τy,τz) ±fx≤μfz,±fy≤μfz4 rows — friction pyramidfmin≤fz≤fmax1 row — unilaterality|τx|≤Yfz,|τy|≤Xfz4 rows — CoP inside the sole±Yfx±Xfy∓μτx∓μτy∓τz≤μ(X+Y)fz8 rows — yaw torque

with μ=0.6, X=0.10 and Y=0.04 m the sole half-length and half-width, fmin=1 N, fmax=104 N. The last group is Caron's closed form for the yaw moment a rectangular patch can carry; crocoddyl builds all seventeen from WrenchCone(R, μ, (2X, 2Y), 4, false, fmin, fmax).

The bracing sites get the 3-D FrictionCone instead — the same first five rows and none of the others, because a point contact carries no moment to bound. That pairing is forced, not stylistic: handing a 5-row friction cone to a 6-D contact does not raise a type error, it mis-sizes an allocation and dies with a bare MemoryError.

The activation is a one-sided quadratic barrier on the cone's own bounds, so the cost is identically zero, value and gradient, anywhere strictly inside the cone:

a(r)=12∑i[max(0,li−ri)2+max(0,ri−ui)2]

Two consequences follow, and they pull in opposite directions. Inside the cone the weight is irrelevant — a plan that never approaches a facet is the same plan at 1e1 and at 1e4. On a facet the weight is the whole story, and it is the only thing standing between the optimiser and a foot wrench MuJoCo will not supply, because to a DDP whose feet are bilateral 6-D welds the robot cannot fall over: the feet can pull, and a centre of pressure outside the sole is free. Session 17 measured what happens when the weight is raised to make that pricing honest, and it is not what one would guess — at 1e2 the controller falls in every seed and at 1e3 it falls from the nominal state. The cone is a stiff, strongly nonlinear function of the state through the KKT solve, and cranking it buys a worse-conditioned problem rather than a safer trajectory.

These costs do not read a force sensor, and that matters for hardware. wc is not measured — it is the contact wrench the OCP's own dynamics produce. DifferentialActionModelContactFwdDynamics solves the KKT system (M,J⊤;J,−dI) for the acceleration and the contact forces together, so wc=wc(x,u) is a function of the state and the control, and enable_force = True exists only so that ∂wc/∂(x,u) is available to differentiate it. Running this controller on the robot therefore needs no contact-force estimate for the cones to be computable. What it does need is the floating-base state and the knowledge that the prescribed contact is actually established — which is a different, and much cheaper, sensing problem. Session 17 measures how much of each.

The keep-out activation

The table term is one CostModelResidual per sampled point, whose residual is the point's position relative to the tabletop box centre and whose activation is

a(r) = ½ · max(0, rmin − sdfbox(r))²

with sdfbox the exact signed distance to an axis-aligned box. It is identically zero — value and gradient — outside the inflated box, so it costs nothing at the certified pose and does not bias the solution the way an always-on half-space penalty does. The point set is one sample per extreme feature of each collision geom of the 8 bodies that can reach the slab (box/mesh corners, capsule endpoints, sphere centres), and every point's threshold is min(rgeom, sdf(q*)) — "do not get closer to the slab than the certified pose already is" — so the constraint is honest about the bounding-box approximation instead of forbidding the pose the study certified. Every point is audited at q* before it is used.

A detail that changes what is being optimised. crocoddyl stores an activation's Hessian Arr as an Eigen diagonal matrix, and the Python binding's setter keeps only the diagonal. So the natural Gauss-Newton term ∇s ∇sᵀ written as np.outer(g, g) was only ever contributing diag(gi²) — the off-diagonal half was discarded silently, by the binding, with no warning. The C++ implementation in §7 writes diag(gi²) explicitly so that it reproduces the existing behaviour exactly rather than quietly solving a different problem.

Getting it to converge

Three things, each of which was a failed solve first:

  1. Warm-start the state by interpolating the whole configuration. Not a constant at x₀ (the line search then spends its iterations at step 2×10⁻³ without leaving the initial point) and not a joint-only interpolation (that swings the legs under a pelvis that never descends and lifts both feet 0.39 m off the floor, violating the very contacts the problem is built on). pin.interpolate over the full q, which also does the right thing on the base quaternion. With a return phase the guess has to come back.
  2. Clamp the warm-start control into the torque box. quasiStatic() on the interpolated guess — a deep lean with the brace not yet down — returns 2.9× the clamp basis for the three-contact mode. SolverBoxFDDP projects the control onto its box before using it, so those entries become a rollout the guess was never evaluated at: cost 4686 against 132 for the guess itself, no trial step accepted, and the run ends after 17 iterations with an uninitialised stop of 9e−310. That signature has been blamed on the cones, on the impulse node and on the terminal weight in this study. It is an initial control outside the bounds the solver enforces.
  3. Two-stage continuation on the cones. On the interpolated guess the cone barriers evaluate at ~3150 against ~2.8 for everything else — three orders of magnitude of the objective is a constraint the guess was never going to satisfy, and FDDP's regularization walks 10⁻⁸ → 10⁹ and gives up. Solving with the cones OFF converges in a handful of iterations and produces a trajectory with plausible contact forces; the cones then enter as a correction. Stage 1 is not a throwaway — it is the only thing that makes stage 2 well-posed.

5 · The ladder: does closing the loop help?

S12 compared "position tracking" against "torque inversion" and concluded that a tracking controller was missing. Neither of those closes a loop around the base, so the comparison could not answer the question. The Riccati gains stabilise; they do not refine. They are computed about a trajectory whose contacts are rigid and prescribed, so where the real robot ends up 30 mm from the planned configuration the LQR simply holds it there. Riccati's error is a bias — a steady 100 mm offset it holds tightly. The MPC's is a transient: it starts the braced phase off-target and converges, and adding the dwell term (which tells the plan to arrive, stop and hold rather than pass through) cuts both the mean and the spread by more than half.

5.1 · Which half mattered — the plan fix or the loop?

Both, and neither substitutes for the other. Running the same three controllers on the S12 plan and on the S13 plan separates them cleanly:

6 · The receding-horizon MPC, in full

The MPC rung is the one that does the work, so it is worth being precise about what it is — and about what it deliberately is not.

6.1 · Construction

It is a receding horizon over the plan's own action models. Nothing is re-authored for the online problem: the models the window slides across are the same objects ocp.build() produced for the offline solve, with the same landing costs, the same reach target, the same cones and the same keep-out. That is the whole point — this measures what re-solving from the measured state buys, not what a different cost function buys.

Step (every 20 ms, i.e. every plan node)What happens
1 · read the plant xmeas = (mj_to_pin(qpos), mj_to_pin_v(qvel, Rbase)) — the full state including the floating base, which is the loop no rung below this one closes
2 · slide the window problem.circularAppend(models[head], datas[head]) until the window covers [k, k+H). Near the end the window would run past the last model and circularAppend can only rotate, never shrink, so the tail rebuilds a shrinking ShootingProblem instead (see the box below).
3 · set the initial stateproblem.x0 = xmeas
4 · warm start first call: the OFFLINE PLAN's own (xs, us) over this window — one DDP iteration from a constant guess is not an MPC, it is noise. Afterwards: the previous solution shifted by one node, with the last entry repeated.
5 · solve SolverBoxFDDP, 1 iteration. Small on purpose: a warm-started DDP taking one or two iterations per control step is the standard construction, and it is what makes the per-step time reportable rather than embarrassing.
6 · command τff = u0, clipped to the torque box, AND (qdes, vdes) = x1 — the MPC re-plans the reference as well as the feedforward, and its own next predicted state is a better setpoint for the servo than the offline plan's. That is what "refinement" means here.

What reaches the robot is still (qdes, kp, kd, τff), identical in form to every other rung. The MPC is not a different interface — it is a different way of filling in two of the four fields, at 50 Hz, from the measured state.

The bug that made "long horizon" mean the opposite of what it says. circularAppend rotates a problem's model list; it cannot make it shorter. Left alone, a window that reaches the end of the plan simply stops sliding — the MPC keeps re-solving a stale set of models while the robot advances past them, and for H equal to the full horizon it never slides at all, which is not "MPC with a long horizon" but "restart the entire maneuver every 20 ms". Measured, that configuration stands still and misses the target by 856 mm, which reads exactly like a horizon-too-long result and is not one. Rebuilding a shrinking problem for the last H steps costs one allocateData per step over that tail only, and took H = 100 from 51 mm to 1.4 mm.

6.2 · What it is not

  • Not real-time. 38 ms per step against a 20 ms control period, after the speed-up in §7 (119 ms before it). Everything on this page is an offline trajectory-generation result; §7 says where the remaining factor of two lives.
  • Not a controller with its own model. It inherits the plan's contact schedule, so it cannot decide to establish a contact the plan does not have, or to let one go. §5.1 is the ablation that shows what that costs: on the S12 plan, whose brace hovers in the air, the MPC reaches 25 mm and still measures 0 N of brace force on every node. It re-plans the motion, not the mode.
  • Not warm-started from nothing. The first call uses the offline plan. An MPC that had to find this maneuver from a cold start in one iteration would not.

Half a second of lookahead is myopic on a 4-second maneuver: the window's terminal model is just the plan's running model at k+H, so the MPC has no value function beyond it and walks the robot into a state the remaining trajectory cannot recover. The full-horizon end fails the other way — one DDP iteration per step is not enough to keep a 200-node problem converged as the state drifts. One to two seconds is where both pressures are satisfied.

The honest timing row. 119 ms per step against a 20 ms control period is 6× too slow. It is also a Python-in-the-loop number: 86 box-SDF keep-out activations are evaluated per node per iteration through the pybind boundary, which is most of it, and the keep-out is a planning term the online problem does not need — the reference it is tracking already avoids the table. Dropping it, and running the C++ solver directly, is the obvious path and is not attempted here. This page claims that closing the loop is what makes the maneuver survive, not that this implementation is deployable.

7 · Making it faster: the keep-out in C++

The keep-out is the OCP's only geometric constraint on the table, and it is 86 activations evaluated per node — 17 200 evaluations per sweep of the 200-node problem, each one an interpreter round-trip into a Python subclass of crocoddyl.ActivationModelAbstract. That is the term that made both the offline solve and the MPC step slow, and none of it is doing anything a compiled function cannot.

lean_analysis/croco_ext/ is a Boost.Python module holding a C++ transcription. It is one translation unit; build.sh compiles it against the same libcrocoddyl and libboost_python the crocoddyl bindings loaded, which is what makes bp::bases<ActivationModelAbstract> resolve at import. croco_geom.activation() returns the C++ object when it is built and the Python one otherwise, and CROCO_KEEPOUT=python forces the interpreter path — which is how the speed-up below is measured rather than asserted.

OperationPython activationC++ activationSpeed-up
problem.calcDiff, 200 nodes181.4 ms109.2 ms1.7×
MPC step, H = 50, 1 iteration118.8 ms40.3 ms2.9×
offline solve, elbow+forearm+palm, both continuation stages 36.5 s12.6 s2.9×
problem construction0.48 s0.47 s1.0×

The two solves are the same solve, which is the point: 38 iterations, cost = 31.5, stop = 2.07×10⁻⁵ from both, to every digit printed. Construction does not move, which is the tell that the cost is in evaluation and not in allocation — the opposite of the earlier circularAppend finding, where allocation was the whole problem. The two fixes compose: fresh problem per step with Python activations was 336 ms, rotated problem with Python activations 119 ms, rotated problem with C++ activations 38 ms.

The transcription is checked, not trusted. croco_ext/test_keepout.py drives both implementations over 2 000 random residuals plus every face, edge and corner of the box and points 1 nm either side of each — the branches of the SDF a random sample never hits — and compares a_value, Ar and diag(Arr). Worst disagreement across all of them: 0.0, bit-for-bit. A speed-up that quietly changes the optimisation problem would invalidate every number this study has already published against the Python activation, so agreement is a test and not a remark.

The remaining 38 ms is still 2× the control period, and the honest reading is that it is not where the effort should go next: this is an offline trajectory generator, and the plans it produces are inputs to MJPC or to an RL policy rather than something that has to close a 50 Hz loop itself. If it did have to, the obvious move is that the online problem does not need 86 keep-out points — the diagnostic says fewer than a dozen are ever active — and pruning to the active set is a much larger factor than any further micro-optimisation of the activation.

Two things worth knowing if you build a crocoddyl extension this way. (a) calc/calcDiff take Eigen::Ref<const VectorXd> because that is the base class's signature, and binding those directly does not work: eigenpy's converter for a const Ref hands Boost.Python a temporary whose storage is gone by the time the call is made, and the symptom is a stack-smash abort with no traceback, not a type error. Thin const VectorXd& thunks fix it, and the solver's own calls never go through them. (b) A C++ derivative inherits the abstract base in the Boost.Python registry but none of its methods, because crocoddyl registers those on its Python wrapper subclass — so createData() on your object raises an ArgumentError naming a class you have never heard of. It only matters for direct Python calls, which is to say it only matters for the test.

8 · Stand → lean & reach → return

A release is a contact removal, which is the one mode transition a prescribed-schedule DDP handles for free: the brace contacts leave the problem, the state regulariser flips from q* back to the start pose, and the CoM barrier comes back because the robot is on its feet alone again. One extra detail was needed — without it the DDP smooths across the phase boundary and starts leaving before it has arrived, missing the target by 81 mm — so the last 35 braced nodes carry the terminal reach weight and a velocity regulariser: arrive, stop, hold, then leave.

9 · How much disturbance does it absorb?

Two disturbances, both applied to the S13 plan. A horizontal force on the pelvis held for 0.2 s at t = 1.4 s — mid-approach, leaning, feet-only support — and Gaussian noise on the 27 initial joint angles. The push is backwards, away from the table, because that is the direction the plant is already weakest: the planned centre of pressure rides the heel edge of the sole (x = −0.100 m on a ±0.10 m sole) through the early approach.

Why a force and not a velocity kick. The first version of this experiment added a velocity impulse to the floating base. It measures almost nothing: both feet are on the ground, the impulse is absorbed within a step, and 0.5 m/s changed the reach error by 1 mm. A force in newtons is also directly comparable to stability.max_push, the study's existing "how hard can something shove this pose" metric.

11 · (contact mode) × (reach target)

Everything above is one target — x = 0.905 m, which the S11 static envelope says the robot reaches on its legs alone. So "the brace carries 107 N and the hand lands 2.2 mm from the target" is a true statement about a task that did not need a brace, and it cannot distinguish a controller that works from one that is along for the ride. This section runs the grid: four targets along the axis that makes the task hard, seven contact modes, and all three stages — static certification, crocoddyl plan, closed-loop MuJoCo — scored the same way in every cell.

9.1 · Choosing the modes: physics, not enumeration

There are 25 subsets over {elbow, forearm, palm, hip, torso} and croco_modes.py will enumerate all of them. Most are not things a robot would ever do, and a matrix padded with them measures the enumerator. The ladder here is chosen from how a brace is actually established — progressively, one contact at a time, and never in an order that requires a link to pass through the table on the way:

9.2 · A defect the single-cell study could not see

Running the grid immediately produced nine cells whose closed-loop replay collapsed with 0 N of brace force — the S12 "leaning on thin air" failure again, but this time upstream of the plan. The cause is in the certification, and it had been there the whole time:

  • contact_select.load() inflates every geom's collision margin by 25 mm so the IK can see a collision before entering one. That is correct and necessary — without it the non-penetration constraint is infeasible in 245 of 250 iterations.
  • But the IK's placement test — "is this site's body in the table's contact list?" — is then evaluated at the inflated margin. A brace up to 25 mm clear of the wood passes it.
  • And the static QP cannot notice: it places its force at the site regardless of whether MuJoCo would report a contact there.

So a pose could be certified, planned against, and handed to a replay that finds nothing under the arm. Two fixes, both in croco_modes.py:

  1. The placement test is now the narrowphase's, at zero margin, and it is a proximity test rather than a body-identity one. Body identity is wrong for two of the three arm sites: the palm site hangs off left_magpie_gripper, but the part of the hand assembly that reaches the wood first is the wrist housing and its brace pad on left_wrist_yaw_link — measured over this sweep, a palm brace lands 31–44 mm from the palm site, on the parent link, every single time. A body test calls that "palm: not touching" for a hand that is visibly flat on the table. So a site counts as placed when a real table contact lies within 100 mm of it, with each contact assigned to its nearest site first (the elbow and forearm sites are only 57 mm apart).
  2. A press step. The pose is re-solved against a zero-margin model, warm-started from the pose the first pass found, in 2-iteration chunks, stopping at the first pose that both touches everywhere and penetrates nowhere by more than 10 mm. It has to be stopped early rather than run to convergence: the site sits on the link axis, so "site z = table z" asks for the link half a diameter inside the slab, and pressed to convergence the three-contact mode buries the forearm 17 mm in the wood and the single-elbow mode buries the upper arm 47 mm. A pose that already touches is left untouched — which is why the S13 reference pose this page's headline result uses is unchanged by any of this, and passes the stricter test as it stands (elbow −7.8, forearm −2.0, palm −2.0 mm).

9.4 · What the grid says

  • The brace becomes necessary between x = 1.05 and x = 1.15. Legs-only is admissible at 1.05 and fails at 1.15 — not kinematically (the IK still reaches the target to 2.0 mm) but by balance: the static QP's base residual is 6.7 N at 1.15 and 31 N at 1.25. This is the first result in the crocoddyl track that says bracing buys something, rather than that a brace can be made real.
  • The palm-only band is real and it is narrow, and it closes on torque. Palm is admissible through 1.15 and fails at 1.25 with peak |τ|/τmax = 1.15 — the wrist saturates while the pose is still perfectly reachable. Its static effort climbs 0.77 → 2.34 → 3.77 over the last three targets, an order of magnitude faster than the elbow's 0.70 → 0.74 → 0.81. The intuition that "the band where palm support is what gets you there must be pretty small" is right, and the reason is the weak wrist rather than the geometry.
  • The forearm is NOT free once you have the elbow. This is the ladder's one clear surprise. elbow+forearm is admissible only at the nearest target; at 1.05 and 1.15 the press has to drive the forearm 18 mm into the slab to make both touch, and at 1.25 the forearm simply does not reach the table at all while the elbow is on it. As the reach extends, the arm straightens and the forearm lifts. What the geometry offers instead is the arch — elbow+palm, admissible at every target — which is what the IK's own answer for elbow+forearm was converging toward all along.
  • The cheapest admissible mode is a single elbow contact, everywhere. Static effort 0.70–0.91 across the whole range, against 0.72–2.10 for the arch and 0.77–3.77 for the palm. Adding contacts costs effort here rather than saving it, because each one is another place the arm has to be simultaneously.
  • The three-contact mode is the fragile one. Admissible at exactly one target of the four. It is the mode this page's headline result uses, and the grid is the thing that says that result is not representative — it is the best case.

10.2 · The closed-loop half does not, and the reason is contact establishment

The black dashed regions are the honest ones, and most of them are not the coloured ones. At the end of the braced phase MuJoCo frequently has no arm contact at all, or a different one than the plan assumed — elbow at x = 1.15 ends up on the forearm, and the plan's own site is in the air. Broken out over the whole phase rather than at one instant:

The pattern is sharp and it is not about the target or the controller:

  • Palm-rooted contacts establish and hold. palm at x = 1.05 carries load on 98% of braced nodes; forearm+palm's palm on 95%. These are the cells whose achieved region matches the certified one (+141 vs +126 mm, +142 vs +176 mm).
  • Elbow-rooted contacts mostly do not. Elbow duty runs 0%, 4%, 5%, 6%, 15%, 31%, 36% across the grid. The plan puts the site where the certification said, holds it there to under a millimetre (§4 — brace_dz is 0.3–1.0 mm on every cell), and MuJoCo still reports no load.
  • And the difference is the contact surface, not the plan. The palm's real contact patch is the wrist housing's brace pad — a flat 85 mm capsule deliberately added to the MJCF to bound the underside. The elbow's is the upper-arm capsule's curved side. A flat pad on a flat table tolerates a millimetre of pose error; a cylinder tangent to a plane does not.
So the honest summary of the grid is narrower than §5's. The S13 headline result — a three-contact brace at 107/57 N that survives — is real, reproduces, and is the best cell, not a typical one. Across the grid the plan reaches the target and holds the brace geometry to a millimetre, and the contact still fails to establish in most elbow-rooted cells. The remaining plan-to-plant gap in this pipeline is not the trajectory and it is not the controller: it is contact establishment on curved geometry, and neither the OCP (rigid prescribed contacts) nor the MPC (inherits the schedule) can see it. That is a concrete next thing to fix, and §13 says how.

13 · What is still wrong

  • Contact establishment is the real remaining gap. Over the grid, elbow-rooted braces carry load on 0–36% of braced nodes while the plan holds their geometry to under a millimetre (§12). The contact model cannot see this: crocoddyl's contacts are rigid and prescribed, so the OCP is free to draw force through a site that MuJoCo will not load, and the MPC inherits the schedule rather than choosing it. A complementarity or contact-implicit formulation is the structural fix; a cheaper one is to plan a deliberate press phase — a short normal-force target on each site before the braced phase begins — and to prefer sites whose contact surface is a flat pad over ones whose surface is a curved capsule.
  • The certification's placement test was wrong until this session and may still be loose. §11.2 replaced a 25 mm-margin body-identity test with a zero-margin proximity one plus a press step. The press is a chunked line search with an early stop, not a solve of the right problem: the right problem is an IK whose site objective is "this link's surface touches the plane", and this study's site objective is still "this point on the link's axis reaches the plane".
  • The plan still dips the gripper 13 mm into the slab at t = 0.22 s. The keep-out is a quadratic penalty, not a constraint, and 13 mm of violation costs 0.07 against a total cost of 31. A hard constraint (SolverIntro / ConstraintModelManager) is the right fix.
  • The elbow brace runs at 0.98 of its friction cone (95th percentile, over the braced phase) on a μ = 0.6 assumption nobody has measured. The cone is a soft penalty here too.
  • q* is still stance-inconsistent. The certified pose's feet are 19 mm narrower than stand's and 6 mm lower; a rigid foot contact cannot be in two places, so 0.65 rad of the ‖q − q*‖ residual is that mismatch rather than a planning failure. The certified pose should be re-solved with the feet where the start pose puts them.
  • The MPC is 2× too slow, down from 6× (§7), and the next factor is pruning the keep-out to its active set rather than any further work on the activation itself.
  • The far targets do not converge. At x = 1.15 and 1.25 the offline solve hits the 200-iteration cap with stop at 6e−3 to 0.17. The reach errors are still sub-millimetre, so this reads as a tolerance rather than a failure — but it has not been chased.
  • One stance, one arm, one table height. The grid moved the target; it did not move the feet, swap the bracing arm, or change the table. All three are cheap to sweep and none has been.
  • The MJPC comparison is still not in this page. The 2026-08-04 handoff result was measured with the MJPC binary, which is not built on this machine; the honest comparison — MJPC's sampling planner against this pipeline on the same target — is the next thing to run.
